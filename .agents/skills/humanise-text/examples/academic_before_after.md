## AFTER (human-rewritten)

Gut bacteria talk to the brain. We've known this vaguely since the early 2000s, but only in the last four years has anyone shown how, precisely, the conversation works -- and what goes wrong in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's patients.

The mechanism is chemical, not electrical. Bacteria in the colon produce short-chain fatty acids (butyrate, propionate, acetate) that cross the intestinal wall, enter the bloodstream, and reach the brain within hours. Once there, they calm microglial cells -- the brain's immune sentinels -- and reduce the chronic inflammation that accelerates neuronal death. A 2024 study in *Nature Neuroscience* measured butyrate levels in 312 early-stage Parkinson's patients and found them 40% lower than in matched controls. That's not a subtle difference.

So researchers tried the obvious thing: put the bacteria back. Three clinical approaches have shown early promise. Probiotic cocktails (specifically *Lactobacillus* and *Bifidobacterium* strains) improved cognitive scores by 11% over 12 weeks in a 2025 trial of 89 Alzheimer's patients. Fecal transplants from healthy donors reversed motor decline in a mouse model. And simple dietary shifts -- more fibre, less processed food -- raised butyrate production by 25-30% in healthy volunteers within two weeks.

None of this is settled science. The microbiome contains roughly 38 trillion organisms from over 1,000 species, and we can culture maybe a third of them in the lab. Correlation still haunts the field. Did the Parkinson's patients have low butyrate because their gut bacteria changed, or did their gut bacteria change because of early neurological damage? Nobody knows yet. Metagenomic sequencing has gotten cheap enough to run longitudinal studies that might answer the question, but those studies are three to five years from producing results.

What's clear already is that neurodegeneration isn't purely a brain problem. It starts, at least partly, in the gut. And that changes which drugs we develop, which patients we screen, and how early we can intervene.
